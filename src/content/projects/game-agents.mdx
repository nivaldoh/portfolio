## What you’re looking at

Here four Empire halberd units, which are built to stop cavalry, screen two units of handgunners and a cannon. gpt-6-sol commands six mounted Bretonnian units, and its first reply set the plan: “Advance the cavalry around the south side of the central obstruction to approach the enemy flank.” The recorded path follows it. The gun line broke between 3:06 and 3:56, and the game declared a win at 8:07.

The view behind each batch of orders was a median 5.4 seconds old when the game applied it (3.0 to 13.5 s). The outlines in the replay show that gap, and I count the delay as part of the result. Quoted reasons are what the model wrote in its replies, and they don’t show its internal reasoning.

<Details summary="This battle in numbers">

- **Setup.** Cavalry assault with a stronger enemy: enemy stats raised by 10%, the game’s AI on very hard, unit size scaled to 0.75, a 20-minute limit. gpt-6-sol ran with low reasoning effort and the rules-only prompt, which explains the objectives and the interface but gives no tactical advice.
- **Replies.** The model was asked 84 times. 83 replies were applied (the last one, requested at 8:04, came after the battle had ended). 13 applied replies sent no new orders. Twice the harness refused a reply that broke the interface’s rules and asked again.
- **Orders.** 211 attack, 88 move, 22 ability and 7 behavior orders were sent. The game rejected 10: four Mounted Yeomen Archers moves to unreachable destinations and the four shots queued behind them, one ability the unit doesn’t support, and one order to a fleeing unit.
- **Timing.** Age of the view behind each applied batch, in battle time: median 5.4 s, mean 5.8 s, range 3.0 to 13.5 s across the 83 applied replies.

</Details>

## How it works

The mod is the only interface to the game. It publishes structured state, showing enemy units only while the army can see them, and carries out orders. Nothing reads the screen or moves the mouse. Python controllers exchange JSON with the mod, and a model can command in two ways.

<ControllerModes />

<Details summary="Interface, timing and what the model can see">

- The mod runs on the game’s script tick, a few times a second. It publishes an observation about once a second and after each order batch it applies.
- One request is in flight at a time, and each prompt states how old the previous batch was when the game applied it. Scripted controllers answer within one tick.
- The model sees its own units in full and enemy units only while its army can see them, plus the enemy roster from the lobby. Scoring and setup fields are removed. Values are exact numbers, so this is not the view a human player has.
- Terrain arrives as a coarse grid of blocked 32 m cells, sampled at deployment, and the game does the pathfinding.
- “Acknowledged” means the game accepted an order, which doesn’t mean it was carried out. Rejections are recorded with the game’s reason.
- Each scenario’s setup is declared up front and checked in the game’s lobby, and a rematch replays the same start.

</Details>

## Saving and restoring a battle

Total War can’t save in the middle of a battle. My checkpoint is the game’s own replay file, written mid-battle, plus the script’s random seed. A restore opens that replay, fast-forwards to the saved tick while comparing the engine’s checksum on every tick, then hands back control at normal speed. Below, one saved moment is restored with different orders and plays out in four different ways.

<div class="wide">
  <CheckpointBranches>
    <figcaption class="caption">Recorded positions from six restores of one moment at 4:30, with five different orders: Empire Spearmen and Handgunners (scripted orders, no model) against the game’s Savage Orcs. Two restores, a repeat and a running attack, played the same battle as “Both units attack”, and the repeat matched for 4,184 ticks. The dotted line is the Orcs’ approach before the save.</figcaption>
  </CheckpointBranches>
</div>

The check I approved restores a three-unit battle twice, and once with a changed order. On the current game build, both restores matched all 2,000 planned ticks, and the readings taken through each one (position, men, hit points, ammunition, morale, abilities, clock and script state) equaled the original. The changed restore split from the original soon after the new order, as expected. A separate check covers four saved siege states: eight restores of 2,400 ticks each matched, and all four changed-order controls split.

When I built field towers by hand in a restored siege, supplies and construction changed but the checksums didn’t. The field readings showed the difference, so the siege check requires them alongside the checksums.

I released the save as a small [public tool for players](https://github.com/nivaldoh/wh3-battle-checkpoints): type `save NAME` during a battle, then open NAME from Replays to play on from that moment. The release still targets the earlier game build (25961564), and the port to the current build isn’t released yet.

<Details summary="Reinforcement learning from saved moments">

- **Setup.** A local Qwen3-0.6B with a LoRA adapter picks one of five order batches at a restored moment. A fixed script plays on to the engine’s result, which is the reward.
- **First result.** Trained on the recorded results of the moment above, the adapter chose to attack at a run, and the live game reproduced the win. A control trained on wrong labels chose to do nothing and lost, as expected. Before training the model already put 0.92 of its probability on winning orders, and trained on two moments at once it fell to always picking the second listed option, so this shows that the loop runs end to end, but not that the model learned much.
- **Synthetic test.** I also ran a test with no game, using a fixed rule (fall back if the enemy is within 100 m, otherwise attack) over 200 training and 100 held-out generated positions. The policy collapsed onto attacking within about 12 updates. A revised version reached 0.955 to 0.975 on training positions but 0.75 to 0.78 on held-out positions, short of the planned 0.9, and I paused the experiment.

</Details>

## Comparing ways to control an army

Six ways of controlling an army, in six battles, against the game’s AI on very hard. Every method with a model uses Claude Haiku 5.5 on low reasoning.

<div class="wide">
  <SuiteGrid />
</div>

Model over scripts won 4 of 5 battles and the same scripts alone won 2 of 4, while one model won none of 6. One model given similar tactics advice and no scripts also won none of 6. One battle per pair so far, so this can’t rank them yet. The [full comparison](/benchmarks/total-war/suite/index.html) has every replay, measure and cost.

<Details summary="What each method does and what the results don’t show">

- **One model.** It orders every unit directly.
- **One model, with tips.** The same, plus similar tactics advice worded for direct orders.
- **Three models.** There is one model each for infantry, guns and knights, all playing at the same time, and they can’t talk to each other.
- **Model over scripts.** One model gives standing instructions, and scripts carry them out between its replies.
- **Written rules.** Before each battle, models write frozen rules per unit class. During the battle, a model only suggests targets every 30 seconds.
- **Scripts only.** The same scripts with no model, at no cost.
- **Paired result.** Model over scripts did better than one model on all 5 shared battles. It won 4, and in the fifth both lost and it scored higher (sign test p = 0.0625, the lowest five pairs can give). Against scripts alone it did better on 3 of 4 (p = 0.625), so those two aren’t separated.
- **Not measured.** Model over scripts has never run without its tips, so I don’t know how much of its wins comes from the advice.
- **Coverage.** 32 of 34 planned battles were played, on one game build. The tips battles ran later the same day, at a different code revision.
- **Battles that didn’t play as designed.** In Pincer the enemy block split in all six battles, in Defeat in detail the two waves arrived within 15 s of each other, and in Breakout neither win kept its infantry on one target.
- **Status.** The suite’s check is only proposed, and its design choices are waiting for my review.

</Details>

## Multiplayer across two machines

My PC and a cloud VM played two short test battles through the game’s own multiplayer on 3 November, with scripted moves and no recorded ending. In the first, a move sent from the PC appeared on the VM, and all 553 unit positions compared at 79 shared battle times were identical. No language model has played this way yet.

<Details summary="What makes it hard">

- An order sent from one client’s script must reach every client’s simulation. Creative Assembly’s modding notes warn that game changes made from interface scripts can desync a multiplayer match. The first two battles showed no desync, which is too little to rule it out.
- Agents have to use the lobby and the Start Battle button without a person. An agent working overnight followed the game’s internal command names to the command behind that button, and a Frida trace of one real click confirmed it. Called directly on one client, it replaced that click once, while the lobby still ran on scripted menu clicks. Two of the four launches in that session that reached the menus stalled, cause unknown.
- No client may pause or slow the battle while a model thinks, and the slowest client can slow everyone.
- Each order is sent at most once. If an acknowledgement is lost, the effect is unknown and the order is not resent.
- Times from different machines are never compared without a measured offset, and battle time comes from the engine.

</Details>

## How I build it

I run the project with Claude Code and Codex. The agents write most of the code, and I set the questions and requirements and decide how each answer gets checked. One question behind the benchmark is whether a model that loses a battle didn’t know the tactic or knew it and acted too late while the battle kept moving.

- A feature counts as working only at a revision where a check I’ve approved passed. Passing tests and single battles are evidence but aren’t enough.
- A check has to fail on a deliberately wrong version before it counts. The tests are replayed against 357 planted bugs. The last full replay caught 355, and the two misses are still open.
- Agents aren’t allowed to edit an existing test in the same change as the code it judges, and the gate lists such edits for review. A separate reviewer agent sees the diff and the raw output but never the author’s explanation.
- Choices an agent makes without me are marked in the code until I decide them.

Ten wrong versions of the pre-commit gate and its hooks once passed every test, and new tests now catch all of them. “Pauses” in restored battles turned out to be clock jumps in WSL (24 of 24 gaps), so delays and view ages now use the engine’s battle clock.

<h2 id="slay-the-spire">Slay the Spire: full runs</h2>

A model plays complete Ironclad runs via CommunicationMod, and a run can end an hour after the bad choice that caused it. At each decision it sees a summary of the public game state, the legal choices labeled one by one, its recent decisions and its own notes. When a recorded run shows a mistake, I change one part of the prompt, memory or interface and run again.

<RunProgress class="wide" />

No run has won yet, and some of the mistakes came from the interface. With the Gambling Chip relic, the game asks at the start of a fight which cards to discard and redraw, but the prompt said only “Choose up to 99 card(s) from your hand”, like any other card choice. On floor 42 of one run the model selected Impervious+ “to block the Transient’s full 30 damage and avoid dying this turn”, and the card went to the discard pile. The newest prompt names what asked for the selection, but it hasn’t had a full run yet.

<Details summary="Interface, pilots and plans">

- CommunicationMod exposes the public game state. The draw pile is shown unordered unless the Frozen Eye relic is held, and the model gets no seed, random-number state, or card and relic text.
- Runs use the Ironclad at Ascension 0 with gpt-6-luna on low reasoning effort. The prompt, memory and interface are on their tenth version, each changing one thing, and the furthest run was on version 8.
- The first-combat pilots compared a scripted heuristic, PPO and a LoRA fine-tune of Qwen3-0.6B on fixed floor-1 fights. The LoRA matched the heuristic’s average remaining HP on a three-encounter development set (76.33 against 76.00, over 12 attempts), but PPO changed its weights without improving play.
- Next for Slay the Spire: restoring a run to a recorded state with the same random numbers, to try other choices from the same point.

</Details>
