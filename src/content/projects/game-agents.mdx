## What you’re looking at

Halberds are built to stop cavalry. Here four Empire halberd units screen two units of handgunners and a cannon. gpt-6-sol commands six mounted Bretonnian units, and its first reply set the plan: “Advance the cavalry around the south side of the central obstruction to approach the enemy flank.” The recorded path follows it. The gun line broke between 3:06 and 3:56, and the game declared a win at 8:07.

The view behind each batch of orders was a median 5.4 seconds old when the game applied it (3.0 to 13.5 s). The outlines in the replay show that gap. I count the delay as part of the result. Quoted reasons are what the model wrote in its replies, not a view of its internal reasoning.

<Details summary="This battle in numbers">

- **Setup.** Cavalry assault with a stronger enemy: enemy stats raised by 10%, the game’s AI on very hard, unit size scaled to 0.75, a 20-minute limit. gpt-6-sol ran with low reasoning effort and the rules-only prompt, which explains the objectives and the interface but gives no tactical advice.
- **Replies.** The model was asked 84 times. 83 replies were applied (the last one, requested at 8:04, came after the battle had ended). 13 applied replies sent no new orders. Twice the harness refused a reply that broke the interface’s rules and asked again.
- **Orders.** 211 attack, 88 move, 22 ability and 7 behavior orders were sent. The game rejected 10: four Mounted Yeomen Archers moves to unreachable destinations and the four shots queued behind them, one ability the unit doesn’t support, and one order to a fleeing unit.
- **Timing.** Age of the view behind each applied batch, in battle time: median 5.4 s, mean 5.8 s, range 3.0 to 13.5 s across the 83 applied replies.
- **Not typical.** In this scenario gpt-6-sol with low reasoning won 2 of its 9 recorded battles. This one is shown for the maneuver.

</Details>

## How it works

The mod is the only interface to the game. It publishes structured state, showing enemy units only while the army can see them, and carries out orders. Nothing reads the screen or moves the mouse. Python controllers exchange JSON with the mod, and a model can command in two ways.

<ControllerModes />

<Details summary="Interface, timing and what the model can see">

- The mod runs on the game’s script tick, a few times a second. It publishes an observation about once a second and after each order batch it applies.
- One request is in flight at a time, and each prompt states how old the previous batch was when the game applied it. Scripted controllers answer within one tick.
- The model sees its own units in full and enemy units only while its army can see them, plus the enemy roster from the lobby. Scoring and setup fields are removed. Values are exact numbers, so this is not the view a human player has.
- Terrain arrives as a coarse grid of blocked 32 m cells, sampled at deployment. It is a hint, not a path planner.
- “Acknowledged” means the game accepted an order, not that it was carried out. Rejections are recorded with the game’s reason.
- Each scenario’s setup is declared up front and checked in the game’s lobby, and a rematch replays the same start.

</Details>

## Save a battle, try another future

Total War can’t save in the middle of a battle. My checkpoint is the game’s own replay file, written mid-battle, plus the script’s random seed. A restore opens that replay, fast-forwards to the saved tick while comparing the engine’s checksum on every tick, then hands back control at normal speed. Below, one saved moment, restored with different orders, plays out four different battles.

Two restores, a repeat and a running attack, played the same battle as “Both units attack”; the repeat matched for 4,184 ticks. The dotted line is the Orcs’ approach before the save.</figcaption>
  </CheckpointBranches>

The check I agreed on restores a three-unit battle twice, and once with a changed order. Over 2,001 ticks no checksum differed, and the readings taken through each restore (position, men, hit points, ammunition, morale, abilities, clock and script state) equaled the original. The changed restore split from the original at the new order, as it should. Three siege cases passed a separate check. All of this is on one game build.

I released the save as a small [public tool for players](https://github.com/nivaldoh/wh3-battle-checkpoints): type `save NAME` during a battle, then open NAME from Replays to play on from that moment.

<Details summary="Reinforcement learning from saved moments">

- **The loop.** A local Qwen3-0.6B with a LoRA adapter picks one of five order batches at a restored moment. A fixed script plays on to the engine’s result, which is the reward.
- **What worked.** Trained on the recorded results of the moment above, the adapter chose to attack at a run, and the live game reproduced the win. A control trained on wrong labels chose to do nothing and lost, as it should. Before training the model already put 0.92 of its probability on winning orders, and trained on two moments at once it fell to always picking the second listed option. So this shows the loop runs end to end, not that the model learned much.
- **What didn’t.** A synthetic test with no game: a fixed rule, fall back if the enemy is within 100 m, otherwise attack, over 200 training and 100 held-out generated positions. The policy collapsed onto attacking within about 12 updates. A revised version reached 0.955 to 0.975 on training positions but 0.75 to 0.78 held out, short of the planned 0.9. The experiment is paused.

</Details>

## Next: agents as teammates

Two agents can already split one army on one PC: each commands its own units and sees its teammate’s units, never its orders. The scripted team passed its live check in 4 of 4 launches; a team of language models hasn’t played yet.

The next step uses the game’s own multiplayer: one game client per agent, each limited to what its player can see on that client. My code reads each client’s view and sends that client’s orders; the game carries the battle between clients.

<Details summary="What makes it hard">

- An order sent from one client’s script must reach every client’s simulation. Creative Assembly’s modding notes warn that game changes made from interface scripts can desync a multiplayer match. If they do, the fallbacks are the game’s own order queue or a command log replayed on every client.
- No client may pause or slow the battle while a model thinks, and the slowest client can slow everyone.
- Each order is sent at most once. If an acknowledgement is lost, the effect is unknown and the order is not resent.
- Times from different machines are never compared without a measured offset; battle time comes from the engine.
- The game on a Windows cloud machine with a GPU is untested.

</Details>

## How I build it

I run the project with Claude Code and Codex. The agents write most of the code; I decide what to build and how each part is checked.

- **Nothing counts as working until a check I agreed on passes**, and only at the revision where it passed. Passing tests and single battles are evidence, not proof.
- **A check must fail on a deliberately wrong version before it counts.** The tests are replayed against 341 planted bugs; the last replay caught all of them.
- **Agents may not edit an existing test in the same change as the code it judges**; the gate lists such edits for review. A separate reviewer agent sees the diff and the raw output, never the author’s explanation.
- **Choices an agent makes without me are marked in the code** until I decide them.

It catches real problems. One night, 10 wrong versions of the pre-commit gate and its hooks passed every test; new tests now catch all 10. “Pauses” in restored battles turned out to be clock jumps in WSL (24 of 24 gaps), so delays and view ages now use the engine’s battle clock.

<h2 id="slay-the-spire">Slay the Spire: full runs</h2>

In Slay the Spire a bad choice can end a run an hour later. A model plays complete Ironclad runs via CommunicationMod: at each decision it sees a summary of the public game state, the legal choices labeled one by one, its recent decisions and its own notes. When a recorded run shows a mistake, I change one part of the prompt, memory or interface and run again.

<RunProgress class="wide" />

No run has won yet.

Some mistakes came from the interface, not the model. With the Gambling Chip relic, the game asks at the start of a fight which cards to discard and redraw, but the prompt said only “Choose up to 99 card(s) from your hand”, like any other card choice. On floor 42 of one run the model selected Impervious+ “to block the Transient’s full 30 damage and avoid dying this turn”, and the card went to the discard pile. The newest prompt names what asked for the selection; it hasn’t had a full run yet.

<Details summary="Interface, pilots and plans">

- CommunicationMod exposes the public game state. The draw pile is shown unordered unless the Frozen Eye relic is held, and the model gets no seed, random-number state, or card and relic text.
- Runs use the Ironclad at Ascension 0 with gpt-6-luna on low reasoning effort. The prompt, memory and interface are on their tenth version, each changing one thing, and the furthest run was on version 8.
- The first-combat pilots compared a scripted heuristic, PPO and a LoRA fine-tune of Qwen3-0.6B on fixed floor-1 fights. The LoRA matched the heuristic’s average remaining HP on a three-encounter development set (76.33 against 76.00, over 12 attempts), but PPO changed its weights without improving play.
- Next for Slay the Spire: restoring a run to a recorded state with the same random numbers, to try other choices from the same point.

</Details>
